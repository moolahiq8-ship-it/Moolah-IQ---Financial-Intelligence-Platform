Two restaurants sit on the same street. One has twelve reviews. The other has two hundred, and it gets found first every single time.

The one with two hundred is not the better restaurant.

It is the one that remembered to ask.

That gap is the whole business opportunity in this build. It is not a marketing problem and it is not a quality problem. It is a timing problem, and timing problems are the kind you can solve with a system.

## Why happy customers never review

Think about the last time a business asked you for a review.

It was probably a line printed on a receipt you had already folded into your pocket. Or a sign by the register you glanced at while counting change. You meant to do it. Then you drove home, and Tuesday happened, and the review never got written.

The owner never knew. Nothing about that was your fault.

The ask arrived at the exact moment your hands were full and your attention was somewhere else. Most happy customers never leave a review, and almost none of them are withholding it out of spite.

Change the moment and you change the outcome. Same customer, same goodwill, but the ask now arrives three hours later, in a pocket, as one tap.

That is the entire product.

## Why reviews decide who gets found

Google's own documentation states that review count and review score feed local ranking. Ranking drives who shows up when somebody searches for a plumber at nine at night.

So a business with twelve reviews is less findable, not only less reassuring. It sits below the fold on the map, and most people never scroll.

This is why the system is worth a monthly fee. The result is a number the owner can watch climb, sitting on Google where they already check it obsessively.

## Step 1: Get the link that opens the review box

Every claimed Google Business Profile carries a short link that opens the write-a-review box directly. No searching, no scrolling, no sign-in wall. One tap straight to the stars.

That link lives inside the Business Profile dashboard under the sharing options. Some dashboards bury it behind a button labelled ask for reviews.

If the business has not claimed their profile yet, stop and claim it first. Everything downstream depends on it.

Copy the link once and save it in the client folder. You will use it in three separate places, so paste it somewhere you can find again without hunting.

**You know this step is done when** you tap the link on your own phone and the review box opens in under two seconds.

Why two seconds matters: every extra tap loses roughly a fifth of the people who meant to help. Search results, sign-in screens and scrolling all cost you finished reviews. Fewer taps means more reviews, and that is the whole game.

## Step 2: Build the trigger that knows a job is finished

Do this after the link is saved, never before. A trigger without a link fires a message that goes nowhere useful.

Use a Google Sheet the business already understands. One row per customer, four columns: name, phone number, service date, and a status column.

Keep the column names short and lowercase. A typo in a column header costs you an evening.

Almost every owner has used a spreadsheet. Almost none has used an automation tool. That difference matters more than it sounds.

In Make.com, add the watch rows trigger. The moment a status flips to complete, the scenario wakes up holding that customer's row. It carries the name and the number, which is everything the message needs.

If the client already runs an appointment reminder system, connect this to that same sheet instead of building a second one. One sheet feeding two builds is easier for you to maintain and easier for the owner to understand.

**You know this step works when** you flip a test row to complete and watch your own name arrive in the run history.

There is a sales reason to build it on a sheet as well as a technical one. A dentist will never open a scenario editor to check on anything. She will absolutely open a spreadsheet, see today's names, and trust what she sees. Show her that sheet on day one and the monthly fee stops feeling abstract.

## Step 3: Add the delay, then send the ask

Turn this on only after the trigger has run clean for a full day. A review request firing on a job that never happened will embarrass your client.

Set the delay to two or three hours after the status flips.

Under an hour feels pushy. Past a day the visit has already faded. Two to three hours is long enough that they are home and settled, soon enough that the visit still feels warm.

Then send one short message, by text or by email, carrying the link.

Write the wording once per client, in the owner's own voice. Something like: thanks for choosing us, a quick Google review would mean the world. Warm, short, no pressure anywhere in it.

Anything colder reads like a debt collection notice, and people ignore those.

### The compliance part you cannot skip

Because this sends texts, United States business messaging rules apply.

Register the client's number for A2P 10DLC business messaging before a single message goes out. Include an opt-out line in every message you send.

Skipping registration gets your client's messages blocked by the carriers. That is not a warning about a fine. It is a warning that the system will silently stop working and nobody will tell you.

**You know this step is done when** a test row produces a text within three hours, and tapping the link from that message opens the review box.

Sit and watch one full cycle before you trust it with real customers.

## Step 4: Add the sentiment gate, and build the honest version

Turn this on once the basic ask has run for a week.

This is the step that separates you from everyone else selling this service.

Instead of sending the review link straight away, the first message asks one question: how did we do today, one to five?

Customers who answer four or five get the Google link automatically.

Customers who answer one, two or three route straight to the owner instead. That owner picks up the phone and fixes the problem while it is still fixable. An unhappy customer who gets a call often becomes the loudest fan later.

That is service recovery. Catching a problem and keeping the customer.

### Build the honest one

Be completely transparent with your client about how this works.

The owner sees every single response, including the ones and the twos. Nothing is hidden, nothing is buried, and no sentiment is ever faked.

The dishonest version of this gate hides complaints from Google and eventually burns the business. Google has removed listings over review manipulation, and when that happens the client loses everything.

It burns you along with it, because your name is on the invoice.

Explain the honest version plainly in the sales conversation. That explanation alone will earn you more trust than any demo. Owners have been burned by agencies before, and honesty is rarer than skill.

**You know this step is done when** a test score of three routes to the owner and a test score of five delivers the review link.

Then test the whole path end to end. Add yourself as a completed job, wait out the delay, tap the link, trigger both branches. When every path fires without you touching anything, the build is finished.
