When you're investing for retirement, choosing between an ETF and a mutual fund can seem like the first big decision. But the label doesn't tell you everything you need to know.

Two funds could own similar investments yet have different costs and purchasing rules. Two ETFs could follow very different strategies. The useful question is: **What am I buying, what will it cost, and can I use it in my account?**

**The short answer:** ETFs trade during the day at market prices. Mutual fund orders receive the next calculated share price. Both can hold stocks or bonds, follow an index or use active management, and lose money. Compare the actual funds—not just their labels. [1]

## The differences at a glance

- **Buying and selling**
  - ETF: Trades on an exchange through a brokerage
  - Mutual fund: Bought or redeemed through the fund or an intermediary
- **Pricing**
  - ETF: Market price changes during the trading day
  - Mutual fund: Next calculated net asset value, usually after the market closes
- **Starting amount**
  - ETF: Usually one share, or a fraction where supported
  - Mutual fund: Minimum varies by fund and account
- **Costs to investigate**
  - ETF: Expense ratio, bid-ask spread, brokerage and account charges
  - Mutual fund: Expense ratio, any sales charges, transaction and account charges
- **Investment approach**
  - ETF: Can track an index or be actively managed
  - Mutual fund: Can track an index or be actively managed

These are starting points. Read the fund's prospectus and your account's terms for the details. [1–4]

## What do you actually own?

Both structures pool investors' money to purchase investments. Your shares represent a portion of the fund's portfolio.

An **index fund** describes a strategy, not a separate alternative to ETFs and mutual funds. Either structure can follow an index. Either can also use active management. [1]

For a meaningful comparison, start with two funds serving a similar purpose. Comparing a broad stock fund with a narrow industry fund mixes a strategy decision into the structure decision.

## Trading, pricing and NAV—in plain English

An ETF's trading price can move throughout the market day. It also has a net asset value, but its market price may sit above or below that value. [2]

**Net asset value, or NAV, is the fund's assets minus its liabilities. Divide that amount by outstanding shares to get NAV per share.**

A mutual fund order generally receives the next calculated NAV per share. A Tuesday-morning order would ordinarily receive Tuesday's closing calculation if it meets the applicable cutoff. [1]

For retirement saving, consider whether trading during the day is a feature you need. Availability alone doesn't make frequent trading part of a useful plan.

## Why a small fee deserves attention

The **expense ratio** expresses a fund's annual operating expenses as a percentage of its average net assets. Those expenses are paid from fund assets, reducing what remains for investors. They generally don't arrive as a separate bill. [3]

On a hypothetical balance that stays near $10,000:

- A 0.05% expense ratio is approximately **$5 per year**.
- A 0.50% expense ratio is approximately **$50 per year**.

Other charges may apply. Mutual funds may have sales charges or transaction fees. ETFs have a bid-ask spread—the difference between quoted buying and selling prices—and may have brokerage charges. Account costs matter for either. **Commission-free doesn't mean cost-free.** [2–3]

The SEC illustrates long-term fee effects with a hypothetical $100,000 investment growing at 4% annually before fees for 20 years. An annual fee of 0.25% leaves about $208,000; a 1% fee leaves about $179,000—a gap of roughly **$29,000**. [5]

This is a comparison of fee levels, **not a guaranteed difference between ETFs and mutual funds**. Neither balance predicts an investor's actual outcome.

The video also applies the same simplified method to one $10,000 deposit over 30 years:

**Annual fee: hypothetical ending balance**

- 0.05%: About $31,951
- 0.50%: About $27,906
- Difference: About $4,045

Assumptions: constant 4% annual growth before fees; annual fee deducted after that year's growth; no additional contributions, taxes or other costs. Calculation: $10,000 × [1.04 × (1 − annual fee)]³⁰. Actual returns fluctuate, and real fund expenses are not necessarily deducted in this simplified annual pattern.

## Start with the account you have

An employer retirement plan gives you a specific investment menu. Review that menu and its plan-level charges before deciding which fund structure you want. A brokerage IRA may offer a broader selection; check the provider's actual offerings.

Keep account type separate from fund type. The SEC notes that the ETF-versus-mutual-fund tax distinction doesn't apply when comparing them inside the same tax-advantaged account, such as a 401(k) or IRA. The account's own tax rules still apply. [1]

## Can you start with $50?

Check rather than assume. A mutual fund may have a starting minimum that fits your budget—or one that doesn't.

An ETF generally requires enough to buy a whole share unless your brokerage supports fractional purchases of that ETF. If a share costs $200, a $50 purchase could buy one-quarter of a share before fees. Eligibility, minimum order amounts and execution rules vary. [4]

Before choosing, verify that your account supports the routine you want: recurring dollar purchases, automatic dividend reinvestment and an affordable contribution amount. These features need checking for the specific fund and provider.

## The label doesn't settle the risk

Look inside the fund. What does it hold? How concentrated is it? What strategy does it follow?

A fund spread across industries has different exposure from one concentrated in a single industry. Diversification can reduce dependence on individual investments, but it cannot prevent all losses. Your time horizon and ability to tolerate losses also matter. [6]

Neither structure guarantees a particular retirement balance. A low fee is useful information, but it isn't a substitute for understanding the investment.

## Four checks before choosing

Choose two funds with a similar purpose, open their prospectuses, and write down:

1. **Account:** Can I buy this fund in the account I intend to use?
2. **Holdings:** What will I own, and what risks come with it?
3. **Total costs:** What are the fund, trading and account charges?
4. **Practical fit:** Can I meet the minimum and invest automatically?

You don't need a universal winner between ETFs and mutual funds. You need a comparison you understand and a routine you can maintain.
