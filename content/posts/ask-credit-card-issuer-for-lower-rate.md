You can ask your credit card issuer for a lower interest rate. You do not need a long speech or a company to call on your behalf. Start with your current rate and one clear question: **Is a lower purchase APR available on my account?**

The issuer may say no. But the conversation can help you understand your options and, if there is an offer, what it actually changes.

Carrying a balance says nothing about your character. An interest rate is a term of your account, and asking about it is an ordinary customer request.

## The words to use

Here is an opening you can adapt:

> “Hi, I'd like to ask whether a lower purchase interest rate is available on my account. My current rate is 22%. Before you begin a review, would it require a hard credit check?”

Replace 22% with your own rate. It is an example, not a market average.

The credit-check question matters. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that hard inquiries can affect credit scores, while soft inquiries, including lenders' reviews of existing accounts, do not. Ask the representative which process applies before proceeding. [CFPB: What is a credit inquiry?](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-inquiry-en-1317/)

## Before you call: gather four facts

Open your latest statement and find your **purchase APR**, the annual percentage rate that applies to purchases. Write down:

1. Your current purchase APR.
2. The balance you carry.
3. How long you have had the account.
4. Whether your recent payments have been on time.

Keep the summary accurate and short. You can ask for a particular rate or simply the lowest purchase APR available on your existing account.

If you have a competing card offer, read its terms before mentioning it. An advertised starting rate does not mean you qualify, and a temporary promotional rate is different from an ongoing rate. You can still make the request without a competing offer.

## Make the request

Call the number on the back of your card. Use the opening above, then add a brief reason if it is true for you:

> “I've had this account for 4 years and have made my recent payments on time. I'm reviewing my borrowing costs. Is there a lower purchase interest rate available?”

Then give the representative time to answer.

If you have a target in mind, you can name it: “Could you offer 17%, or is there another lower rate available?” Those numbers are an illustration, not a prediction of what any issuer will offer.

You do not need to threaten to close the card. A calm, specific request keeps the conversation on the rate itself.

If the first representative cannot help, ask politely whether another team can review the account. Keep the conversation focused on the rate, and do not agree to an account change you do not understand.

## Ask what the offer actually covers

Before accepting, write down the answers to four questions:

1. **What is the new rate?**
2. **Does it apply to my existing purchase balance, new purchases, or both?**
3. **When does it start, and how long does it last?**
4. **Are there fees, account restrictions, or other conditions?**

If the offer is temporary, ask what rate applies afterward. If it is variable, ask how it can change.

A promotional reduction and an ongoing reduction can start at the same rate but have different costs over time. Ask for the terms in writing so you can review them before agreeing, where possible.

## If the answer is no

A no is information, not a verdict on you. Ask one follow-up question:

> “What would need to change for my account to qualify, and when would it make sense to ask again?”

The answer may give you a next step or a useful date to revisit the request. Write it down with the date of the call.

## If paying is getting hard

If you are struggling to make the minimum payment, explain that directly and ask about payment assistance or a hardship program.

The CFPB recommends contacting your card company promptly. Be prepared to explain why the minimum is difficult, what you can afford, when you expect to resume normal payments, and how long you need a different payment arrangement. [CFPB: What should I do if I can't pay my credit card bills?](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-if-i-cant-pay-my-credit-card-bills-en-1697/)

Ask about the proposed payment, interest rate, duration, and fees. Also ask:

> “Would this freeze or close my card, and how would the arrangement be reported to credit bureaus?”

Request written terms. If you need more help, the CFPB also points to credit counseling organizations; ask about their services and costs before signing up. Be cautious of companies that guarantee they can eliminate your debt. [CFPB guidance on payment difficulties and debt relief](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-if-i-cant-pay-my-credit-card-bills-en-1697/)

## After the call: confirm what changed

If you accept an offer, keep a record of:

- The call date and representative's name or reference number.
- The new rate and the balances it covers.
- Any fees, restrictions, or other conditions.
- The effective date and, if applicable, the end date.

Request written confirmation through the issuer's usual channel, such as a secure message or letter. Then check the statement covering the effective date. If it does not reflect the agreed terms, contact the issuer with your notes.

Continue making required payments unless the issuer has agreed to a different arrangement.

## What a lower rate changes

A lower APR can reduce interest on the balance it applies to, with other factors unchanged. It does not erase the debt or necessarily lower your required payment. Your total cost still depends on the balance, payments, new spending, fees, and how long the rate lasts.

The routine is straightforward: **know your rate, ask clearly, check the terms, and verify the change.** Start by finding your purchase APR on your latest statement.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does asking for a lower rate hurt my credit score?**

The question itself is not a credit inquiry. Before authorizing a review, ask whether the issuer will make a hard inquiry. Hard inquiries can affect scores; soft inquiries do not. [CFPB explanation of credit inquiries](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-credit-inquiry-en-1317/)

**Can I ask without a competing offer?**

Yes. Ask whether a lower purchase APR is available on your existing account. You do not need to frame the request as matching another card.

**Is a promotional rate the same as an ongoing reduction?**

A promotional rate may lower your APR temporarily. An ongoing reduction has no stated promotional end date, although a variable rate can still change. Ask when the offer ends and what applies afterward.

**What if I cannot afford the minimum payment?**

Contact your issuer promptly and ask about payment assistance. Explain what you can afford and how long you expect the difficulty to last. Review the terms of any arrangement before accepting it.
