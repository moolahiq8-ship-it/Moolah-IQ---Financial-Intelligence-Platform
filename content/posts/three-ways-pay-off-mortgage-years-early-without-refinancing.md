**The short answer:** you can shorten a 30-year mortgage by years without touching your rate. Add one-twelfth of your monthly payment to every payment so you make the equivalent of a 13th payment a year, send a written request to cancel mortgage insurance the moment your balance reaches 80% of the home's original value, and if a lump sum arrives, ask for a recast by name instead of a refinance. On a $400,000 loan at 6.5%, the first move alone ends the loan about 5.8 years early and saves roughly $116,000 in interest.

## Move 1: The 13th payment, and the word that ruins it

Paying a mortgage down faster is usually treated as a question of willpower. It is closer to a question of timing. Interest is charged on whatever balance remains, so lowering it sooner cuts every month after. Which is why two schedules that sound almost identical produce different outcomes.

**Bimonthly** means twice a month: 24 half payments, which is the 12 whole payments you already make.

**Biweekly** means every fourteen days: 52 weeks divided by two is 26 half payments, and 26 halves is 13 whole payments. That thirteenth payment is the entire mechanism.

### What it is worth

Take a $400,000 loan at 6.5% on a 30-year term. Principal and interest run about $2,528 a month.

| | Standard schedule | With a 13th payment |
|---|---|---|
| Payoff | 30 years | 24 years, 2 months |
| Total interest | About $510,000 | Just under $394,000 |

That is roughly $116,000 in interest you never pay, and 5.8 years you get back.

### The check that decides whether this belongs at the top of your list

The prize scales with your rate, because a higher rate means more interest available to compress. On the same $400,000 loan over 30 years, the 13th payment buys back roughly:

- **3% rate:** 3.5 years
- **6.5% rate:** 5.8 years
- **7% rate:** 6.3 years
- **15-year term at 6.5%:** 1.8 years, because that loan already did most of the compressing

So compare your mortgage rate against what a savings account pays you today. If your rate is higher, every extra dollar of principal effectively earns you that rate. If it is lower, that dollar does more work sitting in the account.

This fork is real: 51.5% of outstanding U.S. mortgages still carried a rate at or below 4% as of Q3 2025 (Realtor.com analysis of the FHFA National Mortgage Database). The next two moves work at any rate.

### The obstacle is the servicer, not the math

Many servicers will not accept a half payment as a payment. They hold it in what they call suspense until a full payment arrives. Your money sits there for two weeks earning nothing, the balance never drops early, and so the interest never drops either. Other servicers will sell you a biweekly program to solve a problem you can solve for free. Ask what the enrollment fee is first, because it is a charge for arithmetic.

**Do it yourself instead.** Take your monthly principal and interest, divide by twelve, and add that amount to every monthly payment. On the loan above, that is about $211. Write "extra principal" on the memo line, or select "principal only" in your servicer's portal.

That produces the same thirteenth payment with none of the suspense risk, and keeps the monthly rhythm your bank account already knows. Then call your servicer once and ask two questions. Does extra money apply to principal, or to next month's payment? Does the note carry a prepayment penalty? Most conventional loans carry none, but hear it said out loud, and write down who told you and when.

**You know it worked when** next month's balance sits below what your original amortization schedule predicted. Pull the schedule from your closing packet and compare the balance line.

## Move 2: The insurance line, and the two dates that behave differently

Open your statement and find the line for mortgage insurance. If you put less than 20% down it is probably there, reading either PMI or mortgage insurance premium. On a $400,000 loan it commonly runs $150 to $500 a month.

That charge is not permanent, and removing it is not a favor. The Homeowners Protection Act gives you three separate exits, and all three require you to be current on payments.

### Exit one: yours to trigger

You may request cancellation once your principal balance reaches 80% of the home's original value. The servicer can require a good payment history, evidence the property has not declined in value, and no second lien.

This is the exit that rewards Move 1. According to Consumer Financial Protection Bureau guidance, the request date can rest on the original amortization schedule **or on any later date your actual balance reaches the threshold.** Every extra dollar from Move 1 pulls that date closer.

### Exit two: automatic, and indifferent to your effort

Your servicer must terminate the insurance automatically when the balance is first *scheduled* to reach 78% of the original value. The CFPB is explicit that this date comes from the amortization schedule, irrespective of your outstanding balance. Extra payments do not move it at all.

The two exits behave in opposite directions, which is exactly why the letter matters.

**What that gap is worth.** On a loan starting at 90% of value, the 80% request date arrives around month 95. The automatic 78% date arrives around month 109. Fourteen months apart. At a $250 monthly premium, that is $3,500 for one letter.

Notice the word letter. The Act specifies a written request. A phone call alone does not start the clock.

### Exit three: the backstop

Your servicer must end the insurance the month after the midpoint of your loan's amortization schedule, whatever the balance says. On a 30-year loan that is month 181.

### How to actually do it

Send the request in writing and keep a copy. Ask what documentation they require and whether they want a new appraisal, which runs a few hundred dollars and should be weighed against the monthly premium. Get a confirmation number and effective date in writing, then check the next two statements.

**One caution, because FHA loans work differently.** Under HUD Mortgagee Letter 2013-04, for case numbers assigned on or after June 3, 2013, borrowers cannot request cancellation of annual MIP by paying down principal. If your original loan-to-value was 90% or less, meaning roughly 10% or more down, the annual premium ends after 11 years. Above 90% original LTV it generally lasts the life of the loan, and the exit is a conventional refinance.

**You know it worked when** your payment drops while your rate and term stay identical.

## Move 3: The recast, for anyone holding a lump sum

Ask most people what to do with a windfall and they say refinance. If your rate sits well below what lenders offer today, refinancing trades away the one thing you cannot buy back, and closing costs on a $400,000 loan are commonly quoted in the $5,000 to $15,000 range.

There is a quieter option that keeps your rate exactly where it is. It is called a **recast**, and most servicers do not advertise it: you make a lump sum payment toward principal, and the lender re-amortizes the remaining balance across your remaining term.

Same rate. Same payoff date. Smaller payment. A one-time fee typically in the $150 to $500 range, with no appraisal and no credit inquiry.

### The obstacle is that you have to ask by name

Many servicers will not mention a recast when you call about a large payment. Some will take the lump sum and leave your payment unchanged: the balance drops, the payoff date moves in, the monthly bill stays the same. That is not wrong, and for some people it is better. But it should be your decision rather than one made for you.

**So decide before the money moves.** Want the payoff date to move in? Send the lump sum and keep paying the old amount. Want room in the monthly budget instead? Ask for the recast by name and get the fee in writing.

Two rules before you call. Most servicers set a minimum lump sum, commonly in the $5,000 to $10,000 range, and require you to be current. Government-backed loans generally cannot be recast, so FHA and VA borrowers should ask about applying extra principal instead.

And check one thing in the same call: if that lump sum pushes you below 80% of original value, send the written cancellation request too. Move 2 stacks on top of Move 3.

**You know it worked when** the next statement shows a lower payment at the identical rate.

## Three moves, no new loan

A rate check, a letter, and a question nobody told you to ask. The largest bill in your life is worth an afternoon.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is biweekly the same as bimonthly?**
No. Bimonthly means twice a month: 24 half payments, or the 12 whole payments you already make. Biweekly means every 14 days: 26 half payments, or 13 whole payments a year. Only biweekly creates the extra annual payment.

**Do extra principal payments make my PMI drop off sooner?**
Partly. They can pull forward the date you become eligible to *request* cancellation at 80% of original value, because that request can rest on your actual balance. They do not move the automatic 78% date, which comes from the original schedule regardless of what you owe. That is why the letter is worth sending.

**Should I recast or refinance?**
They solve different problems. A refinance changes your rate, restarts the term, and costs thousands. A recast keeps your rate and payoff date and lowers the monthly payment, usually for a few hundred dollars. If your rate is below what lenders offer, a recast preserves it and a refinance gives it away.

**Can FHA borrowers remove mortgage insurance by paying down the balance?**
Generally no, for loans with case numbers assigned on or after June 3, 2013. If your original loan-to-value was 90% or less, annual MIP ends after 11 years. Above that, it typically lasts the life of the loan, and refinancing into a conventional loan is the usual exit.
